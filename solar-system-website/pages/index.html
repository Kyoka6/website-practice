Our solar system is a dynamic family of worlds

The Sun and its orbiting bodies formed from a collapsing protoplanetary disk about 4.6 billion years ago. Differences in temperature, material composition, and collision history produced rocky inner planets, gas and ice giants, and diverse moons with radically different geologies and atmospheres.

Fun fact

The Sun contains about 99.8% of the solar system's mass, so its gravity dominates orbital motion and sets the baseline energy environment for every planet.

Fun fact

Jupiter's mass is about 318 Earth masses; that huge gravity well strongly perturbs asteroids and comets and has helped shape the long-term architecture of the solar system.

Fun fact

Neptune, despite receiving little sunlight, can drive winds above 1,500 km/h, showing that internal heat and atmospheric dynamics can dominate weather on distant worlds.

Overview

A quick tour of the celestial bodies

Star

The Sun is a G-type main-sequence star powered by core fusion, converting hydrogen into helium and emitting the radiation that drives climates, photochemistry, and energy flow across the system.

Inner worlds

Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars are metal-and-silicate planets with solid surfaces, high mean densities, and geologic records of volcanism, impacts, and tectonic processes.

Gas giants

Jupiter and Saturn are hydrogen-helium giants with deep convective atmospheres, strong magnetic fields, and complex ring and moon systems that act as miniature laboratories for orbital dynamics.

Icy giants

Uranus and Neptune contain higher fractions of water, ammonia, and methane "ices," with unusual circulation patterns and interior structures that differ significantly from the larger gas giants.

Celestial body

The Sun

The Sun is a self-gravitating plasma sphere where hydrogen fusion in the core releases energy that diffuses outward and radiates into space. Its luminosity sets the thermal boundary conditions for planets, while solar wind and magnetic activity influence planetary atmospheres and space weather.

  • It is classified as a G2V main-sequence star.
  • Its core temperature is about 15 million K.
  • Fusion converts about 600 million tons of hydrogen per second.
Type
G-type main-sequence star
Diameter
About 1.39 million km

Celestial body

Mercury

Mercury is an iron-rich terrestrial planet with a disproportionately large metallic core and a very thin exosphere instead of a dense atmosphere. Because heat is not efficiently redistributed, day-to-night temperature differences are extreme.

  • Its surface records intense early impact bombardment.
  • A Mercury year is about 88 Earth days.
  • Solar tides and spin-orbit resonance shape its rotation cycle.

Celestial body

Venus

Venus is a terrestrial planet with a dense CO2 atmosphere and sulfuric acid cloud decks that produce an extreme greenhouse effect. Its surface pressure and temperature make it a key case study in climate feedbacks and atmospheric evolution.

  • It rotates retrograde, opposite most planets.
  • Surface pressure is about 90 times Earth's sea-level pressure.
  • Runaway greenhouse warming keeps its surface near 465 C.

Celestial body

Earth

Earth is a differentiated rocky planet with active plate tectonics, a dynamic hydrosphere, and a chemically reactive atmosphere. Its coupled carbon cycle, magnetic shielding, and moderate insolation enable long-term liquid water stability at the surface.

  • About 71% of the surface is covered by liquid water.
  • The atmosphere is mostly nitrogen and oxygen.
  • The magnetosphere helps deflect charged solar particles.

Celestial body

Mars

Mars preserves a geologic archive of volcanism, impact processes, and ancient water flow, making it central to comparative planetology and astrobiology. Its thin CO2 atmosphere supports seasonal weather, dust storms, and active polar processes.

  • Olympus Mons is the largest known volcano in the solar system.
  • Valles Marineris is a giant tectonic canyon system.
  • Iron oxide minerals give the surface its red appearance.

Celestial body

Jupiter

Jupiter is a rapidly rotating gas giant with deep zonal winds, layered cloud chemistry, and a powerful magnetosphere. Its immense gravity has influenced the orbital evolution of small bodies and likely reduced or redirected many potential inner-system impacts.

  • The Great Red Spot is a long-lived anticyclonic storm.
  • Its magnetic field traps intense charged-particle radiation.
  • Major moons like Europa and Io show active geophysics.
Type
Gas giant

Celestial body

Saturn

Saturn is a hydrogen-helium giant with low bulk density and the most optically prominent ring system in the solar system. Its rings and moons provide direct evidence for resonance interactions, tidal sculpting, and long-term orbital migration.

  • Its average density is lower than that of liquid water.
  • Ring structure includes gaps shaped by shepherd moons.
  • Titan has a dense nitrogen atmosphere and methane cycle.

Gas giant

Celestial body

Uranus

Uranus is an ice giant with an extreme axial tilt of about 98 degrees, so its poles alternately face the Sun for long seasonal intervals. Interior heat flow, atmospheric circulation, and magnetic geometry differ in important ways from both Saturn and Neptune.

  • Methane absorption gives the atmosphere its blue-green color.
  • It has narrow rings and many small inner moons.
  • Its rotation axis strongly alters seasonal energy balance.
Type
Ice giant
Moons

Celestial body

Neptune

Neptune is a distant ice giant where internal heat drives strong convection and fast atmospheric jets despite weak sunlight. Its meteorology, chemistry, and moon system help scientists test models of outer-planet formation and evolution.

  • Observed winds can exceed 1,500 km/h.
  • Dark vortices appear and dissipate over time.
  • Triton likely formed elsewhere and was later captured.
Type
Ice giant
Moons

Celestial body

Moon

Earth's Moon likely formed after a giant impact early in Earth's history and has since evolved through tidal interactions. It records impact chronology, volcanic flooding of maria, and space-weathering processes that help calibrate planetary surface ages.

  • Its synchronous rotation keeps one hemisphere facing Earth.
  • Tidal friction is slowly increasing Earth-Moon distance.
  • Lunar regolith stores a long record of solar and impact history.
Type
Natural satellite
Distance
About 384,400 km from Earth
